Program concept · Second draft · Song-first revision  ·  session demo  ·  how to run a session

Cántame

A daily fifteen-minute reading program for Mexican-American children in which the song comes first, the letters come out of the song, and stillness is practiced as carefully as sound.

Learners Heritage Spanish speakers, ages 3–8 Model 1:1, 15 minutes a day, scripted, any trusted adult Vehicle Songs, poems, riddles, rhymes from the Mexican oral tradition Prepared September 2026
In brief

What changed, and why it holds together

The first draft, Cuéntame, put a story at the center and hung songs on it. This draft turns that inside out. The song, the poem, the riddle, and the rhyme are the text. Everything the child learns to read, they first learn to sing or say. The story survives only as a thread: each song is one that Lupe gathers for her grandmother's cancionero.

Three commitments define the revision. First, song is the vehicle, not the reward. A verse the child already carries in their body is the ideal object on which to point out a rhyme, clap a syllable, find where the stress falls, meet a letter, and then read the line in print; the research on nursery rhymes, music programs, and sung text says this is not sentimental but mechanically sound.44,46,49 Second, the literacy blocks stay explicit. Song lifts phonological awareness, memory for text, prosody, and fluency; it does not, on its own, teach letter–sound correspondences, and the best study of a daily ten-minute music program found exactly that gap.46 So every session still contains four minutes of scripted, systematic Spanish phonics, and the letters are drawn from the song. Third, song is used to gather attention, not to seize it. Music can be a stimulant or a settling force depending on tempo, volume, pacing, and how it is used; the program deliberately chooses the settling register (unaccompanied voice, one tune repeated for weeks, scheduled silence, freeze games played slowly) and treats the child's capacity to stay with a phrase as a skill to be trained on a ladder across the year.54,55,59

The working name is now Cántame, "sing to me." The two strands are still marked: Literacy for decisions from the reading science, Cultura for decisions from the research on identity, family, and heritage language, and Both where one design choice serves both. A third concern, attention, runs through all of them and gets its own section.

The case

Why this program, and why now

Spanish is the home language of four million English learners in U.S. public schools, three-quarters of all ELs, and those children are concentrated in the early grades: nearly 15 percent of kindergartners are ELs, compared with 6 percent of twelfth graders.15 Most of their families cannot keep Spanish alive across generations without help. Pew finds that 57 percent of U.S.-born Latinos can hold a conversation in Spanish; among the third generation and beyond it is 34 percent.16 Among Mexican-origin families in Southern California, 36 percent of the second generation spoke Spanish well, 12 percent of the third, 2 percent of the fourth.17 Eighty-five percent of Latinos say it matters that future generations speak Spanish; among Latinos who do not speak it, 54 percent have been made to feel bad about it by another Latino.16

Songs are the part of the language that survives longest. A grandmother who no longer reads Spanish still sings A la rorro niño; a third-generation child who answers in English still knows Los pollitos dicen. Vicente T. Mendoza's Lírica infantil de México catalogued this repertoire in 1951, from lullabies through counting songs to riddles and never-ending tales, organized by the age of the child who sings them.64 It is a curriculum that already exists in the families we want to serve. The reading science and the identity science both say that building on it is sound: learning to read in the home language raises later reading in English,7,9 and children who keep their heritage language and feel good about their ethnic identity show better psychological and academic adjustment.18,19

The chassis

What we keep from Once

Once's operating model is unchanged: one adult, one child, fifteen minutes a day, a fully scripted sequence, two half-day trainings, weekly video coaching, recorded sessions, monthly data meetings, and a dosage threshold (80+ sessions) below which the program does not claim results.1,2,3 The tutoring literature supports every one of those choices: one-to-one tutoring by teaching assistants produces effects (+0.44) at least as large as certified teachers (+0.38),12 paraprofessional tutors average about 0.40 SD across 96 randomized trials, effects are largest in PreK–1, and daily programs beat weekly ones.11

Two things about the chassis change. Once's continuous story becomes a continuous cancionero: a physical songbook that gains one song, poem, or riddle every two weeks, in the child's own hand where possible, so that by the end of the year the child owns a book of thirty-some pieces they can sing and read. And the emotional register of the sessions changes. Once's lessons are designed to be "suspenseful and engaging."2 Cántame's are designed to be steady.

Strand one

Song as the vehicle for the literacy blocks Literacy

Learning to read in Spanish has the same components as in any alphabetic language: phonological awareness, letter–sound knowledge, decoding, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension, with writing woven through. What is unusual about Spanish is how much of the early load falls on the syllable and on stress, and how little on irregular spelling.4,5 Song is unusually well matched to that profile.

What the evidence says song can carry

Rhyme knowledge predicts reading. In the classic longitudinal study, sixty-four children's knowledge of nursery rhymes at age three predicted their reading and spelling three years later, after controlling for IQ, social background, and initial phonological skill; the effect ran through phonological sensitivity.44 For Spanish specifically, preliterate children find rhyme (the end of a syllable) easier to hear than the beginning of a syllable, which makes rhyme the natural entry point for three- and four-year-olds.45

A daily ten-minute music program builds the large units, and only those. Degé and Schwarzer randomized 41 five- and six-year-olds to a music program (singing, drumming, meter, dance), a phonological-skills program, or sports, for ten minutes a day over twenty weeks. Music improved rhyme, syllable segmentation, and blending with an effect of d = 0.9, larger than the phonological program itself (d = 0.6). It did nothing for phoneme-level awareness.46 That result is the design of Cántame in miniature: the same dosage, the same gain, and the same reason to keep explicit phonics in the room. A meta-analysis of thirteen studies puts the average effect of music training on phonological awareness at d = 0.20, growing with hours of training.47

One-to-one rhythm work moves poor readers. In a small trial, poor readers aged six and seven received nineteen one-to-one sessions of rhythmic games: tapping to a beat, clapping while chanting, reciting poetry with attention to stress. Rhyme awareness rose by d = 1.01, nonword reading by d = 0.95, and the children who improved most at keeping a beat improved most at reading (r = .57).48 A Uruguayan study with five-year-olds found the same pattern: rhythm-focused music training beat unstructured singing on phonological awareness, and a year later the music group read more words per minute.67

Melody makes text stick, if the melody repeats. Wallace showed that verses are recalled better sung than spoken, but only when the same simple tune carries every verse; when the melody varied from verse to verse, spoken text won.49 Singing a passage rather than speaking it also improved children's pronunciation and verbatim recall in a second language, with the advantage still present six months later.68 And three-year-olds who heard the same storybook three times learned and retained more words than children who heard three different books with the same words.50 The lesson for a curriculum is unglamorous: one tune, one text, many days.

Stress is part of Spanish literacy. Spanish marks lexical stress in its spelling. The tilde in pío or maíz is a written instruction about where the voice lands, and in Spanish ten-year-olds, prosodic (stress) awareness predicts reading and spelling independently of phonological awareness.51 Song exaggerates stress. A child who has sung ma-ÍZ a hundred times already knows what the accent mark is for.

Repeated reading of a known text builds fluency. Repeated reading has an average effect near 0.75 on fluency, strongest when an adult models the text first and gives feedback.53 The "Poetry Academy" gave struggling third graders one poem a week with a community volunteer (choral reading, gradual release, practice at home) and produced significant gains in words read correctly and word recognition.53 A verse the child can sing is a text they will reread twenty times without being asked.

Bilingual Spanish–English children hold their ground. In a one-year study of 42 low-income Spanish–English bilingual children aged six to nine, those in a community music program held their age-normed reading scores while matched controls declined.52

The register

Song that gathers attention instead of grabbing it Both

Children's media, and much children's music, works by capture: fast cuts, bright color, rising pitch, constant novelty. That register has a measurable cost. Four-year-olds who watched nine minutes of a fast-paced cartoon performed worse on executive-function tasks immediately afterward than children who watched a slow-paced educational program or simply drew.55 Kindergartners taught in a heavily decorated classroom spent 39 percent of the lesson off task and scored 42 percent on the test, against 28 percent and 55 percent in a bare room.56 Children need a five to seven decibel better signal-to-noise ratio than adults to understand speech, and second-language learners are hurt most by background sound.57 Cántame's first attention decision is therefore subtractive: one voice, no backing track, no screen in front of the child, nothing on the table but the cancionero and the syllable cards.

Music has two registers; choose the settling one

The same song sung two ways does two different things to a child's body. When mothers sang a song soothingly, both mother's and infant's physiological arousal fell below baseline as the song went on; when they sang it playfully (faster, higher, louder, with a sharper pulse), arousal held steady and the infant's gaze locked on the mother.54 Playful singing captures; soothing singing settles. Infants also stay calm roughly twice as long listening to singing as to speech.69 The Mexican repertoire already contains both registers, and the arrullo (lullaby) is the oldest and most widely shared of all of it.64 Cántame opens and closes every session in the soothing register, sings the working song at a walking pulse in between, and never uses the playful register to win the child back.

Attention is trainable at this age, and rhythm and song are the tools

Five sessions of attention training over two to three weeks measurably improved executive attention in four-year-olds, whose brain responses afterward resembled untrained six-year-olds'.62 The duration of focused attention grows steadily between ages one and four and a half, driven partly by growing inhibitory control.72 Three lines of music research say how to work on it:

  • Freeze games with songs build self-regulation. "Red Light, Purple Light," a set of circle-time games in which children move to a song or a drum and stop on a cue (and later must do the opposite of the cue), improved behavioral self-regulation in Head Start preschoolers, with the largest gains for children who started lowest; in the first small trial the treatment group also gained on letter–word identification. The What Works Clearinghouse, reviewing the two later trials (433 children, a third of them English learners), rates the self-regulation effect as small but positive (ES = 0.16) and the literacy effects as uncertain.59
  • Keeping a beat trains inhibition, and tracks attention. In 76 five- and six-year-olds, twenty weeks of rhythm-based training (three 20-minute sessions a week) improved inhibition (d = 0.82 versus sports) where pitch-based training did not.60 Among adolescents, the steadiness with which a person can tap to a beat correlates with sustained auditory attention (r = .47) and with reading.61 A teacher-delivered rhythm-and-movement program for preschoolers in disadvantaged communities has shown small positive effects on teacher-rated self-regulation and on the Head-Toes-Knees-Shoulders task in cluster-randomized trials in Australia and Hong Kong, though not on a broader executive-function composite.70
  • Children who sing use song to regulate themselves. Three- and four-year-olds who had taken music classes showed better self-regulation (d = 0.41) and were more likely to sing or hum to themselves while waiting in a delay task; the song had become a tool they reached for.58 That is the outcome Cántame wants: a child who, asked to wait, sings inside.

Six rules of the quiet table

  1. One voice, no track. The adult sings unaccompanied. Audio exists for adults who need to learn a tune, played face-down and off before the child sits.57
  2. One tune, two weeks. Each unit has one song or poem. Novelty is rationed; repetition is the method.49,50
  3. Walking pulse. Working songs are sung at roughly 70–85 beats a minute, kept with palms on the table. Never faster to "wake up" the child.54,60
  4. Quieter to gather. When attention drifts, the adult lowers their voice and slows the pulse. Volume goes down, not up.
  5. Silence is scheduled. Every session contains planned silence: inner singing, a freeze, a riddle's waiting time. Its length grows on the ladder below.59
  6. Praise the staying. The script praises holding still, waiting for the next word, and finishing the verse ("Te quedaste con la canción hasta el final"), not speed or volume.

The attention ladder

Each rung names what the child can do by the end of the phase. The demand rises in three ways at once: the length of silence held, the amount of song carried inside without sound, and the complexity of the stop rule.

Strand two

The repertoire is the cultural strand Cultura

In the first draft the cultural content was a set of themes. Here it is a body of texts. Mendoza organized the Mexican children's lyric by the age at which it belongs to a child: lullabies and nursing rhymes first, then religious and Christmas songs, then the coplas of six- to eight-year-olds, then games and rounds, never-ending tales, and the narrative ballads and cumulative counting songs of older children.64 That order is close to a scope and sequence already, and Cántame follows it.

Arrullos · lullabies

A la rorro niño, Señora Santa Ana, Duérmete mi niño, Este niño lindo. The soothing register in its purest form; sung, never decoded, at the start and end of every session. The first "para la casa" question of the year is "¿Qué arrullo te cantaban a ti?"

Rondas y juegos · rounds and games

Naranja dulce, limón partido; A la rueda de San Miguel; La víbora de la mar; Arroz con leche; Los pollitos dicen. Strong pulse, simple repeated tunes, built-in stop cues; these carry the freeze games and the syllable work.49,59

Coplas, adivinanzas y dichos · verses, riddles, sayings

"Agua pasa por mi casa, cate de mi corazón" (aguacate); "Oro parece, plata no es" (plátano); "Sana, sana, colita de rana"; the family's own dichos. Riddles are the program's comprehension texts and its waiting exercise; sayings are where families contribute most.27,28

Trabalenguas y cuentos de nunca acabar · tongue-twisters and endless tales

"Pepe Pecas pica papas"; "Erre con erre"; "Este era un gato con los pies de trapo." Fluency texts, read slowly and cleanly at the pulse rather than fast; the endless tale is a lesson in knowing when to stop.

Calaveritas y poemas · verses for the dead, and poems

For Día de Muertos the child writes a two-line calaverita for a relative or a pet, in the tradition of the affectionate mock-epitaph.40 In Phase 5, poems: Amado Nervo's Cantos escolares (1903), written to be sung in Mexican schools and now public domain,73 and Las mañanitas.

Palabras con raíces · words with roots

The songs are full of Nahuatl: elote, tomate, aguacate, chapulín, papalote, guajolote.39 One per session, and the rule of thumb that Mexican words ending in -te often came from -tl. Seven million people in Mexico speak one of 68 Indigenous language groups; the child learns that as a living fact, not an ancient one.39

Why this builds love, not just skill

The identity research says the feeling the program is after has real correlates. Positive ethnic-racial affect is associated with higher self-esteem, better achievement, and fewer depressive symptoms across 46 studies of Latino, Black, Native American, and Asian American youth,18 and ethnic identity is already protective in Mexican- and Dominican-American four- and five-year-olds.19 Mexican-American children build the components of that identity between preschool and age ten.20 Heritage-language proficiency predicts ethnic-identity affirmation in Latino adolescents,23 and when the home language is lost, "rifts develop and families lose the intimacy that comes from shared beliefs and understandings."26 Song is the most intimate form of that shared language. Nobody sings A la rorro niño to a child they do not love; a program that puts that song in the child's mouth, and then in their handwriting, is transmitting the feeling with the words.

The thread that remains of the story

Lupe's grandmother is making a cancionero for the family and has lost her voice. Each unit, Lupe learns a song from someone (a neighbor from Puebla, a cousin in Oaxaca, the man who sells elotes) and brings it home; the last unit is a song the child's own family gives. The narrative is two or three sentences a week, read by the adult, and exists to answer the child's question of why we are learning this one next.

Rights, and what "culture" must not become

Traditional songs and rhymes in Mendoza's collection are anonymous and public domain. Cri-Cri (Francisco Gabilondo Soler, 1907–1990), the most beloved Mexican children's songwriter, is not: Mexican copyright runs for the author's life plus 100 years, so his catalog is licensed material and budgeted as such.65 Amado Nervo (d. 1919) is public domain; Gabriela Mistral's Ternura is due to enter the Chilean public domain in 2028, though a bill to extend her term by ten years is pending, so Phase 5 should not depend on it.73 The three guardrails from the first draft stand: no shame for a child who arrives with little Spanish; no single Mexico (rondas from Jalisco are not rondas from Yucatán, and the family's own version of a song outranks the script's); and Indigenous peoples named as living communities.

The year

Scope and sequence

Thirty-six weeks, five phases, one piece every two weeks (three in Phase 1, where the first songs need longer). The phonics order is the consensus order for Spanish structured literacy;6,14 the repertoire follows Mendoza's developmental order;64 the attention rung is from the ladder above.

A day

The fifteen-minute session

Six parts, always in the same order, so a grandfather on day three and a paraprofessional on day ninety run the same session. The shape is an arc: settle, work, stretch, settle. Roughly a third of the time is singing, a third is explicit reading work, and a third is talk and silence.

1min
Arrullo settle
The adult hums the phase's lullaby; the child joins with lips closed. Three slow breaths on the last phrase. Same tune every day of the phase.54

The unit's piece, sung through once at a walking pulse with palms keeping the beat on the table; then one verse line by line, adult first, child echoing.60

From the song: find the rhyme, clap a word's syllables on the pulse, sing the line to find the stressed syllable. Then the scripted phonics: the day's letter from a song word, blend into syllables, build two words with cards, write one.46

The verse in the cancionero. Sing it while tracking with a finger; chant it on the pulse; say it; then read the unit's decodable verse without any tune. Three clean readings, never faster.53

Worked example

Unit 1, session 12: Los pollitos dicen

Week three. The child has sung this song every day for eleven sessions and knows it by heart. Letters taught so far: the five vowels, then m, p, s, l, n. Today's letter is t. The song is public domain (written by the Chilean teacher Ismael Parraguez and published in 1907; it is now sung across the Spanish-speaking world).66 It was chosen as the first song for a reason beyond familiarity: it moves from need (hunger, cold, pío pío pío) to rest (asleep under the wings until morning). The song's own arc is the session's arc.

Los pollitos dicen pío, pío, pío,
cuando tienen hambre, cuando tienen frío.
La gallina busca el maíz y el trigo,
les da la comida y les presta abrigo.
Bajo sus dos alas, acurrucaditos,
hasta el otro día duermen los pollitos.
1 · Arrullo (1 min)

(Humming the tune of A la rorro niño, lips closed, slowly. After one phrase, the adult opens a hand on the table, palm up; the child rests a hand on it. Two more phrases. Then, very quietly:) Tres respiraciones con la canción. (The adult breathes audibly with the last line, three times.)

Attention note: the adult does not say "listen" or "ready." The hum is the cue. If the child talks, the adult keeps humming, softer. The session starts when the child is humming too, or after the third breath, whichever comes first.

2 · La canción (3 min)

Manos en la mesa. (Both begin patting the table slowly, about one pat per second.) Cantamos con las manos. (Sings the whole song once at that pulse, without stopping, eyes on the child, not loud.)

Ahora tú me sigues. Yo canto un pedacito y tú lo cantas igual. "Bajo sus dos alas..." (child echoes) "acurrucaditos..." (child echoes) "hasta el otro día..." (child echoes) "duermen los pollitos."

Families sing this song in several versions ("bajo de sus alas," "les da su comida y les da abrigo"); the family's version wins, and the adult marks it in the cancionero. The echo stanza is always the last one, the quietest. The adult sings each fragment slightly softer than the one before, so the child's echo comes down with it.

3 · Los sonidos (4 min)

Escucha: pí-o... frí-o. Terminan igual. ¿Cuál otra palabra de la canción termina como trigo? (abrigo) Muy bien, riman.

Ahora vamos a aplaudir la palabra pollitos con el pulso: po-lli-tos. ¿Cuántas partes? Cántalo despacio y dime cuál parte suena más larga. (po-LLI-tos) Esa es la parte fuerte.

La letra de hoy es t. Suena /t/, con la lengua en los dientes, suavecito. Está al final de una palabra que acabamos de aplaudir: polli-tos. Y en una palabra que vamos a leer hoy: tapa. Dilo tú: /t/. Ahora con las vocales: ta, te, ti, to, tu. (Cards:) Pon ta y luego pa. Léelo despacio. Tapa. Ahora escríbela en tu cuaderno.

Adult note: an English-style /t/ with a puff of air is common in heritage speakers; model it once with the tongue on the teeth and move on. Correct at most twice. The stress question ("which part sounds longest") is asked by singing, not speaking; the melody is what makes the answer obvious.

4 · Leer la canción (3 min)

Abre tu cancionero en los pollitos. Pon el dedo en la primera palabra. Cantamos despacio y el dedo camina con la canción. (Sing the last stanza, the adult's finger under the child's.)

Ahora sin cantar, solo con el pulso. (Chant the same stanza, patting.) Ahora solo lo decimos. (Speak it.)

Y ahora, sin música, leemos nuestro versito. Este es solo tuyo.

Pío, pío, pío.
Mi mamá me tapa.
Sus alas son mi manta.

Every word in the decodable verse uses only the letters taught through today (vowels, m p s l n t) and the accent the child has sung. In the printed song above it, words with untaught letters (pollitos, dicen, gallina) appear in lighter ink; the child reads them from the song, and each will be decoded when its letter arrives. Three readings of the decodable verse at the pulse. The third is the one that counts: "Leíste todo el versito sin tropezar."

5 · Por dentro (2 min)

Ahora la canción va por dentro. Yo canto la primera estrofa y tú la cantas dentro de tu cabeza, sin voz. Cada vez que oigas pío, tocas la mesa una vez. (Adult sings the first stanza at the walking pulse; the child taps three times on the three píos.)

Ahora estatuas suaves. Yo canto, y cuando me quedo en silencio, tú te quedas como un pollito dormido, sin moverte, y esperas la siguiente palabra. (Sings the last stanza, stops after "Bajo sus dos alas," holds three seconds of silence, then continues with "acurrucaditos." Repeats once with the pause elsewhere.) Esperaste la palabra sin moverte. Eso es escuchar.

Rung for Phase 1: a 3-second pause. The adult counts it silently. The child is never told the pause is coming. If the child fills the silence, the adult does not react; she resumes singing, and next time pauses for two seconds instead of three. The rule is that the child succeeds today; the silence lengthens across the week.

6 · Cierre y para la casa (2 min)

Los pollitos duermen bajo las alas de su mamá. ¿Quién te tapa a ti en la noche? Dímelo con una oración completa: "En la noche me tapa..."

Palabra con raíces: elote. La gallina busca el maíz, y el maíz tierno se llama elote. Esa palabra viene del náhuatl, élotl; los pueblos indígenas de México cuidaron el maíz desde hace miles de años. ¿Qué comes tú que se hace de maíz? (tortillas, elote, tamales, atole)

(Humming A la rorro niño again, one verse, softer than the opening.) Esta noche pregunta en tu casa: ¿qué arrullo te cantaban cuando eras bebé? Mañana me lo cantas, o me lo tarareas.

The closing lullaby is the same every day of the phase and is shorter than the opening one. The session ends in the register it began in. Nothing is said about tomorrow's letter.

Two more examples from later in the year

Phase 2, week 11: a tongue-twister read slowly

Pepe Pecas pica papas con un pico.

Every letter is taught by week 11 (p, c hard, vowels, s, n). The tradition says a trabalenguas is for speed; Cántame uses it for the opposite. The child keeps the pulse alone on the table for ten seconds while the adult is silent, then reads the line three times at that pulse, cleanly. The adult's only feedback is which reading was cleanest. The point is a text hard enough that the child must attend to every syllable, delivered at a tempo that makes attending possible.53

Phase 3, week 16: a riddle with a counted wait

Agua pasa por mi casa, cate de mi corazón.
El que no lo adivine, es un burro cabezón.

The adult says the riddle once, slowly. The rule, taught in Phase 3, is that a riddle is answered only after five fingers have been counted in silence. The child holds the question, holds the answer, and holds still; that is delay of gratification dressed as a game.59 Then the phonological work: agua + cate, the two halves clapped and joined; then the riddle in print, where c before a and z in cabezón are the day's letters; then the word with roots, aguacate, from Nahuatl ahuacatl.39 "Para la casa": ask someone at home for a riddle they knew as a child.

Two channels

School and home

At school

Once's operating model, unchanged: the school assigns paraprofessionals or aides; Cántame supplies the scripts, two half-day onboardings, weekly 30-minute video coaching, session recording, and monthly data meetings.1 The onboarding adds two things. Adults learn the year's repertoire (thirty-odd tunes, most already known to any Mexican-American adult) from audio, and they rehearse the quiet register: singing at a walking pulse, lowering rather than raising the voice, and holding a three-second silence without filling it. A quiet corner, not a classroom wall, is a requirement.56

At home

The adult may be a parent, an older sibling, or an abuela, and for this program the grandmother is often the best-qualified person in the household: she knows the songs. Parent-delivered tutoring averages about half the effect of paraprofessional tutoring,11 so the home channel leans on coaching and on scripts that can be run by an adult who does not read Spanish fluently (audio for every spoken line, played before the child sits down). The National Academies' guidance that children benefit most from adults using the language they are most competent in applies with special force to song.10

Candor

What the research does not say

  • Music does not make children smarter, and the program should not say it does. The most rigorous meta-analysis of music training's far-transfer effects finds them near zero in randomized studies with active controls;63 a re-analysis argues small effects are real.63 Cántame's claims are all near-transfer: song builds rhyme and syllable awareness, memory for text, stress awareness, and fluency on the texts sung, and the explicit phonics does the rest.
  • Song does not teach phonemes. The best daily-dose study found no effect of music on phoneme-level awareness.46 The four minutes of scripted phonics are not optional.
  • The arousal evidence is on infants. Soothing versus playful singing has been measured physiologically in mother–infant pairs,54 not in six-year-olds at a reading table. The freeze-game and rhythm trials are the direct evidence for this age, and their effects are small.59,70
  • Nobody has tested poem memorization in young children. The Phase 5 target rests on the song-memory and repeated-reading evidence, not on a trial of recitation.
  • The identity evidence is mostly adolescent, and correlational for preschoolers.18,19 The program should claim to build identity, and measure whether it does.
  • Once's own outcome studies are vendor-commissioned; the independent tutoring literature is what supports the chassis.3,11
  • Some children will find the quiet register hard at first, and a few, with attention difficulties, will need the ladder to start below rung one. Intake should include a baseline silent span and HTKS, and the script should branch.
  • Not every family sings. The home channel must work for a parent who is embarrassed to sing; audio of the tunes, permission to hum, and a script that says "you can say it instead" are part of the design, not an afterthought.
